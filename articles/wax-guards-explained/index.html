Wax guards and filters: how to buy the right pack for your hearing aid, and why the name on the box is not enough

The wax filter is the smallest part of a hearing aid and the first thing to suspect when one goes quiet. It is also strangely easy to buy wrongly: the same name is stamped on different products, colours change from pack to pack, and the name of your hearing aid does not tell you which filter it wears. This guide explains the two filter families, gives you the identification method that actually works - the earpiece in your ear, not the box on the shelf - and puts honest, dated UK prices on the packs, including the question UK NHS-supplied wearers should ask before paying at all.

What this guide covers. The small replaceable wax filters - also sold as wax guards or cerumen filters - fitted to receiver-in-canal and custom hearing aids: why they exist, how the disc and stick systems differ, how to identify the one your aid uses, how a change is done, when manufacturers say to replace, and what packs cost in the UK. It is written for people who already own their aids and are buying replacement filters, whether the aids came from a private clinic, a high street chain or an NHS audiology department, across the brand families sold in the UK: Phonak and Unitron, Oticon and Bernafon, Signia and Rexton, Widex, ReSound, Starkey. This is an editorial guide, independent of the manufacturers; every product fact in it is sourced, and the sources are listed at the end.

In brief.

  • Receiver-in-canal and custom hearing aids carry a tiny replaceable filter guarding the sound outlet from earwax. A clogged filter is the first thing to suspect when an aid goes weak or silent - and the cheapest thing to fix.
  • There are two families: disc systems, where a rotating disc changes the filter, and stick systems, where a disposable two-pin tool does. The change method is itself the identification.
  • Identify a filter by the earpiece you actually wear and the shape of the pack you use now - never by colour, and never by your hearing aid's platform name. The same aid model can wear either Sonova system depending on the receiver fitted.
  • Names mislead: at least three different products trade under the name CeruStop. Always pair the filter name with the manufacturer and check the shape.
  • Manufacturers split on timing. Phonak's own guide schedules replacement - "should be replaced every four weeks on a regular receiver" - while Bernafon's instructions give no calendar for the filter: replace when clogged.
  • Indicative UK prices observed 6 August 2026: single packs run from about 3.95 to 6.95 GBP at online consumables specialists, against 12.95 at clinic-attached shops for the same families. If your aids are NHS-supplied, ask your audiology department before buying: policies vary by trust.

Why a part this small exists at all

A receiver-in-canal hearing aid keeps its loudspeaker - the receiver, on the end of the thin wire - inside your ear canal, which is exactly where earwax is made. Wax that reaches the speaker's sound outlet does not damage it dramatically; it simply blocks it, and the aid goes quiet in a way that feels like a breakdown. The wax filter is the defence: a small replaceable guard seated over the sound outlet, there to be sacrificed. When it clogs, you replace the filter, not the speaker.

That is why this consumable earns a guide of its own. A blocked filter is the most ordinary failure a hearing aid has, the fix costs a few pounds, and manufacturers design the swap to be done at home - Bernafon's troubleshooting table answers no sound and reduced sound with the same instruction, replace the wax filter, dome or Grip Tip. It is also a part the warranty does not cover: Bernafon's instructions for use exclude "accessories such as batteries, tubing, speakers, earpieces and filters" from the warranty in so many words. Filters are yours to buy for the life of the aid, which makes buying the right ones, at a fair price, worth five minutes of understanding.

One note on words before anything else: manufacturers say wax filter, wax guard, waxguard or cerumen filter, and they all name the same part. This guide says filter throughout.

The two families: disc systems and stick systems

Nearly every current system is a stick system. The filter is a tiny cartridge seated in the speaker tip or earpiece, and each fresh pack contains disposable applicator tools: a slim stick carrying two pins, one empty, one pre-loaded with a new filter. The empty pin pulls the old filter out; the loaded pin pushes the new one in; the tool is thrown away. Sonova's CeruStop, Oticon and Bernafon's ProWax and ProWax miniFit, Signia and Rexton's NanoCare 3.0, Widex's NanoCare, Starkey's HearClear and the GN guard fitted to ReSound aids all work this way - but each family's filter and tool are sized to that family, so the shared principle never means a shared part.

The disc system stands alone: Sonova's CeruShield Disk, used on Phonak and Unitron aids fitted with the SDS 4.0 receiver generation. There is no loose tool. One rotating disc holds eight fresh filters; the earpiece comes off and the speaker tip clicks into the disc itself - once to shed the used filter into the disc, once to pick up a new one. The used filters stay stored inside, which is the system's point: nothing fiddly to grip. The operator-reviewed catalogue behind this guide records that Sonova introduced the later stick generation after the disc was found fiddly by some users, which is why both systems remain on sale side by side.

Which filter your aid uses: read the earpiece, not the box

The rule this guide hangs on: the filter is decided by the earpiece and receiver actually fitted to your aid - never by the aid's platform name, and never by the colour of anything. The receiver on the end of the wire is chosen by the audiologist at fitting or repair, and the same aid model can leave the clinic with different receiver generations. Sonova is the clearest case: a Phonak or Unitron aid wearing an SDS 4.0 receiver uses the CeruShield Disk, while one wearing an SDS 5.0 or 6.0 receiver - or one of the older two-pin receivers on earlier Phonak and Unitron models - uses CeruStop sticks. Two aids with the same name on the box can genuinely need different packs. The reliable check is what you use now: a disc means the disc system; a stick tool means CeruStop.

Colour deserves its own warning, because it is the identifier most people reach for first and it lies in both directions. Packs of the same product ship in different colours by region and seller, and many filters are deliberately sold in a range of colours - black, brown, tan, white - so the part blends against the body of the aid. Colour encodes aesthetics and geography, not identity. The one colour that does mean something is the ear-side marking - red for right, blue for left - and even there it marks which ear, never which filter type: Signia's older HF-series packs use red and blue holders on both HF3 and HF4 formats, so colour alone cannot separate even those two.

The family map, brand by brand

Where filters do cross brands, it is inside a corporate family - no sharing beyond one is verified, and the Widex case below shows it is not automatic even within one. Oticon and Bernafon - both Demant companies - share the ProWax system: a standard speaker worn with a dome or Grip Tip takes the user-changeable ProWax miniFit, while custom moulds carry the ProWax filter, and Bernafon's own instructions add that with a mould or LiteTip fitting a hearing care professional replaces the speaker's filter. The sharing is stated by the manufacturer itself: Bernafon's Coral instructions for use name ProWax miniFit as their filter, the same system Oticon documents. A few earpiece types - Bernafon names its VarioTherm micro mould and LiteTip - carry no filter of their own at all.

Signia and Rexton, both WS Audiology brands, share the current NanoCare 3.0 guard, and Audio Service's G-series RIC aids take the same part under Audio Service's own branding. Signia's older HF3 and HF4 formats are separate products: not interchangeable with each other, and not interchangeable with NanoCare 3.0 - a pack marked HF3 or HF4 must be matched exactly. ReSound aids take GN's own guard, sold under the SureFit name at retail, and Danalogic - GN's UK NHS brand - has no filter of its own: it uses the same GN part. Starkey's HearClear serves the Starkey family, Audibel, NuEar and MicroTech included, and Starkey's own guidance states it plainly: HearClear wax guards are "not compatible with other manufacturer's devices".

Now the trap this article exists to spring before you do. The name CeruStop is not one product: at least three different products trade under it in shop listings. Sonova's CeruStop for Phonak and Unitron receivers is one; GN's guard for ReSound aids is a second, relabelled CeruStop by some resellers though it is GN's own distinct part; and Widex sells a CeruSTOP XL of its own, a third. A search result saying CeruStop therefore tells you almost nothing. The same trap has a second door: Widex's NanoCare and Signia's NanoCare 3.0 share a name and nothing else that any manufacturer document we could reach establishes - the two belong to different aids, and no first-party source equates them, so never buy one for the other's aid on the strength of the name. The defence against both is the same and it is this guide's method: match the manufacturer of your aid, the shape of your current pack and the earpiece you wear - a bare filter name is never a compatibility signal.

How a change is done

For stick systems the procedure is a minute's work, and Bernafon's Coral instructions state it in three sentences. The tool "has two pins, one empty for removal and one with the new wax filter". Then: "Push the empty pin into the wax filter in the speaker and pull it out." And: "Insert the new wax filter using the other pin, remove the tool and throw it out." That is the whole job - no twisting force, no reused tool. The same document draws the professional line plainly: "If you use a mold or LiteTip, a hearing care professional must replace the wax filter in the speaker" - the spelling is the manufacturer's - so mould wearers hand this job to the clinic rather than buying a retail pack.

The disc system trades the tool for the disc itself. As Phonak's support material describes it - with the rotation step corroborated first-party in Unitron's own user guide - the earpiece is pulled off the speaker, the disc is turned until a free opening sits ready, and the speaker tip clicks into the disc twice: once into a free opening, where the used filter stays behind, and once into an opening holding a fresh filter, which seats itself on the tip. The disc's own markings show which opening does which. Follow the leaflet in your pack for the exact markings; the point here is the mechanism - and that a disc and a stick are never substitutes for one another, whatever a seller's compatibility list implies.

When to replace: two manufacturers, two philosophies

Here honest guidance means reporting a split rather than inventing a rule. Phonak schedules the change: its own guide for the current SDS 6.0 receiver states that the wax protection "should be replaced every four weeks on a regular receiver". Bernafon's Coral instructions, read in full for this guide, contain no calendar interval for the wax filter anywhere in the booklet: the trigger is simply "If the filter becomes clogged, please replace", backed by the troubleshooting table's advice to replace the filter when sound goes weak or dead. One maker gives you a diary date; the other gives you a symptom.

Neither is wrong: ears produce wax at very different rates, so a schedule that suits one wearer wastes filters for another and comes too late for a third. The practical reading of both philosophies together: change the filter when sound weakens, and if you want a diary rule, the only manufacturer calendar this guide can quote is Phonak's four-week figure above - scoped, as Phonak scopes it, to a regular receiver, not extended to any other maker. What this guide will not do is print a universal every-N-weeks figure, because no such figure exists across manufacturers, and the ones that circulate on retailer pages do not trace to primary documents we could verify.

What packs honestly cost

Prices in this section are indicative observations, not offers: they were read from UK retailer search listings on 6 August 2026, they are quoted as displayed including VAT, and they will drift - treat them as a map of the market's shape rather than today's checkout price. Packs themselves are retail configurations, typically six, eight or fifteen filters depending on family; no manufacturer we could verify publishes pack counts as a specification.

The shape of the market is a spread, and the spread is the story. At online hearing-aid consumables specialists, single packs of the major families ran from 3.95 GBP (CeruStop, CeruShield and Signia NanoCare 3.0 alike) through 4.95 (Starkey HearClear) to 6.95 (the Oticon and Bernafon ProWax families). An assistive-listening specialist listed the CeruStop pack at 9.46 including VAT. Clinic-attached shops listed ProWax miniFit and NanoCare 3.0 packs at 12.95 - the same families that the online specialists listed at 3.95 to 6.95. That is up to roughly a three-fold difference on identical consumables - 12.95 against 3.95 on the NanoCare 3.0 pack - which is why knowing exactly which filter you need - by manufacturer and shape - is worth money: it lets you buy from the cheapest shelf with confidence.

And one shelf may cost nothing at all. If your hearing aids came from an NHS audiology department in the UK, ask the department before buying anything this guide describes: batteries and repairs are free on the NHS, departments run consumables services for parts such as tubing and domes, and some cover filters too - but filter policy varies by trust, and no national rule we could verify guarantees it. Worth knowing alongside that: most NHS-fitted aids are behind-the-ear models wearing thin tubes or earmoulds, which do not take the retail filters in this guide at all, so the NHS wearers this section serves are the smaller group fitted with receiver-in-ear models such as Danalogic's. A guide connected to a retailer loses a sale by saying ask-first; it is still the right first stop.

Who should not buy a retail pack

Three groups should stop before the basket. NHS-supplied wearers in the UK, first: ask your department before you pay - batteries and repairs are free on the NHS and many departments cover consumable parts, so a retail purchase may buy nothing but convenience. Second, wearers whose earpiece is a custom mould or LiteTip on an Oticon or Bernafon aid: the manufacturer's own instructions route the speaker's filter change to a hearing care professional, so the retail miniFit pack aimed at dome wearers is not your product - and some mould types carry no user filter at all. Third, anyone who cannot yet match a pack to the shape of what they currently use: with three different products trading as CeruStop and two unrelated NanoCares on sale, buying on a name you half-remember is how the wrong pack arrives. Take the old pack, or a clear photo of your earpiece, to your clinic or to the seller and ask - the question costs nothing and the wrong pack is pure waste.

Questions worth asking before you buy

My hearing aid has gone weak or silent - could it really just be the wax filter?

Yes, and it is the first thing to try after charging or a fresh battery. A wax-blocked filter silences an aid so convincingly that manufacturers put it at the top of their own fault tables - Bernafon's troubleshooting answers both no sound and reduced sound with replace the wax filter, dome or Grip Tip. A filter change costs pennies and a minute; a repair quote costs a courier and a wait. Do the cheap check first.

How often should I change the filter?

The manufacturers themselves split, so any single figure would be an invention. Phonak's guide for its current receivers schedules it: wax protection "should be replaced every four weeks on a regular receiver". Bernafon's instructions give no calendar for the filter - replace when it clogs. Your own wax production decides which philosophy fits: change at the first sign of weakened sound.

My new filters are a different colour from my last pack - have I bought the wrong thing?

Probably not - colour is the one property that proves nothing. The same product ships in different pack colours by region and seller, and many filters are sold in several colours on purpose, to blend against the aid's body. Check the manufacturer name and the shape of the filter and tool against your old pack instead. The only colour that carries meaning is the red-right, blue-left ear marking on some holders - and Signia's older HF3 and HF4 packs both use that red and blue coding, so even there colour marks the ear, never the format.

I wear Oticon and my partner wears Bernafon - can we share filters?

Yes, if you both wear the standard dome-type fitting: both brands belong to Demant and share the ProWax miniFit system - Bernafon's own instructions name ProWax miniFit for its aids. Check that both of you actually use the same shape of pack before merging supplies, because the sharing holds at the system level, not for every earpiece: a custom mould on either aid moves that wearer to the professionally replaced ProWax filter instead.

My fitting card mentions a 60, 85 or 100 speaker - do I need different filters for different strengths?

Not if you wear a dome or Grip Tip on a standard speaker: for those fittings the strengths take the same ProWax miniFit guard, so the number on your card does not change the pack you buy. The number matters at the edges: if your earpiece is a custom mould, the filter question changes with it - mould fittings sit in ProWax territory with professional replacement, so check with your clinic before buying rather than reading the answer off the strength number alone.

My aids are Danalogic from the NHS - do ReSound wax guards fit?

Yes - Danalogic is GN's UK NHS brand and there is no separate Danalogic filter: the part is GN's own guard, the one sold for ReSound aids under the SureFit name. Before you buy it, though, ask your NHS audiology department whether it supplies them - departments cover many consumable parts free of charge, and if yours covers filters, that beats any retail price in this guide.

Widex NanoCare and Signia NanoCare 3.0 - same name, same filter?

No - the shared name is the trap. Widex and Signia both sit under WS Audiology, but no manufacturer document we could reach equates the two products: they are catalogued separately, for different aids, and this guide found no first-party basis for putting one into the other's earpiece. Buy by the manufacturer of your own aid and the shape of your current pack, and treat the matching name as coincidence until a manufacturer says otherwise.
