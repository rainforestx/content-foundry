Phonak Roger On 3: what it does, who it fits, and how the receiver question really works

The Phonak Roger On is a wireless microphone - held, worn or placed near the talker - that carries their voice past noise and distance, straight into hearing aids and cochlear implant processors, Advanced Bionics processors among them. The third generation changed the most confusing part of buying one - the receiver - by shipping an unlimited supply of them inside the microphone. This guide explains what that means, and exactly which devices can use it.

What this guide covers. The current, third-generation Roger On - the unit shipping today. It is written for people who wear hearing aids from Phonak, Unitron or Hansaton on a recent platform, for Advanced Bionics cochlear implant users, and for wearers of other manufacturers' aids weighing up whether Roger can reach their devices at all. Earlier generations appear only where the differences matter to a buyer. This is an editorial guide, independent of the manufacturer; every product fact in it is sourced, and the sources are listed at the end.

In brief.

  • A remote microphone for hearing past noise and distance: held, worn or placed near the talker, it carries their voice by radio into receivers in your hearing devices.
  • The third generation carries unlimited receivers inside the microphone and installs them itself into compatible Phonak, Unitron and Hansaton aids and the AB Naida CI M and Sky CI M processors.
  • Other manufacturers' aids connect instead through a Roger NeckLoop (any T-coil device) or a Roger X receiver.
  • Phonak's datasheet: 27 grams, USB-C charging, IP54, and a stated 8 hours of streaming to RogerDirect devices on a charge.
  • Not for devices with no T-coil, no direct audio input and no RogerDirect platform - confirm your model's path before spending anything.

What it is, and how it works

Hearing aids are short-range instruments. Phonak's own professional literature puts the useful conversational range - the "near field" in which directional microphones and noise reduction do their best work - at about 1.5 metres from the talker. Beyond that, the aid's microphones take in more room and less voice, and no amount of amplification fixes a signal that arrives already buried in noise.

A remote microphone attacks the problem at the source. The Roger On is worn or placed near the person you want to hear. It picks the voice up close to the mouth, then transmits it by radio to receivers in your hearing devices, so the voice arrives at your ears at close-talker quality regardless of where you are sitting. Phonak's demonstration guidance for the product describes hearing a talker at up to 25 metres where background noise is present.

The radio side is Phonak's own Roger protocol, not Bluetooth. It runs in the licence-free 2.4 GHz band, and to survive congestion there it sends every packet of sound three times over, on different channels. This matters practically: an aid's ordinary Bluetooth connection to a phone is not a Roger connection, and the two should not be confused when working out compatibility.

A note on the name. Sonova's corporate materials brand the product simply "Roger On"; the "3" marks the third generation, and appears in Phonak's own user-guide documentation as well as in retail listings. This guide uses "Roger On 3" where the generation matters and "Roger On" where it does not.

The receiver, and what changed in the third generation

A Roger microphone is a transmitter. On its own it does nothing - the sound has to land in a receiver on your side, and the receiver question is where most buyers get lost (our dedicated receiver guide treats it in full). Launched by Sonova in August 2024, the third generation simplifies it considerably: Roger On 3, along with Roger Select 3, Roger Table Mic 3 and Roger Touchscreen Mic 3, ships with unlimited RogerDirect receivers built into the microphone itself. If your hearing aids are on a compatible platform, you hold them next to the microphone once, the receiver installs wirelessly into each aid, and there is no separate receiver to buy, and nothing extra to wear. Phonak publishes no cap on how many devices one microphone can serve this way, and the connection survives restarts: in the words of Phonak's user guide, "Connecting a receiver is only required once. The hearing aid / Roger receiver will remain connected with your microphone even after the hearing aid / Roger receiver has been restarted."

Earlier units do not have this. The previous Roger On and Roger On iN can occupy the same shelf and a similar price, so the distinction is worth holding onto: Sonova's launch material is explicit that the unlimited built-in receivers and the associated new features are only available in the Roger 3 generation. The iN units carried built-in receivers to install, but not the unlimited pool; non-iN units relied on separately purchased receivers installed by a hearing care professional.

Living with it: the modes, and what they are for

The Roger On is Phonak's all-situations microphone, and its modes map onto placements. It contains an accelerometer - the same class of component that rotates a phone screen - and uses orientation plus the acoustic environment to pick its microphone mode automatically: held in the hand, lying on a table, or clipped to a lapel each produce different behaviour without a menu visit. If it is dropped, it mutes on the way down, so the listener is spared the crash of the landing.

Two Roger On capabilities are easy to miss in a shop demonstration. The first is stereo wide pointing mode, controlled from the myRogerMic phone app, which widens the pickup into a broader field - Phonak's professional material gives a panel discussion as the natural case, one microphone placement covering a row of talkers. Stereo delivery carries a condition, covered under compatibility below. The second is the MultiTalker Network: the Roger On can be combined with other Roger microphones so a larger meeting or a moving conversation stays covered; when it joins a network of microphones it is worn by a talker rather than pointed. Phonak's technical datasheet puts the network at up to ten connected microphones.

Day to day the automation carries most of the load. Roger microphones also adjust their level with the environment - as noise rises, the system's volume rises with it, keeping the talker above the noise floor without the listener reaching for a control.

The practical numbers, from Phonak's own technical datasheet for the third-generation unit: it weighs 27 grams, charges by USB-C, and is protected against splash and dust to IP54. The built-in rechargeable battery runs a stated 8 hours when streaming to RogerDirect hearing devices, or 6 hours through a separate Roger receiver - enough for a school or working day on one charge, but worth knowing before a long day that runs into the evening. In the box, per the same datasheet: the microphone, the docking station, a power supply with USB cable, optical and analog audio cables for the TV connection, a lanyard and a case.

Compatibility, precisely

Every compatibility question about this product reduces to one question: what receiver path do your devices have? There are four, and they cover different populations.

Path 1: RogerDirect, the built-in route

Phonak hearing aids on the Infinio, Lumity and Paradise platforms - the current ranges, including the flagship Audeo Infinio Ultra and Sphere Infinio - include RogerDirect, as does the Marvel platform, an earlier generation still supported. The signal streams from the microphone straight into the aids, with no adapter and nothing worn. Phonak's own literature carries one fine-print exception worth knowing about: a handful of Virto custom in-ear models (Titanium and certain no-wireless variants) sit outside RogerDirect and take the T-coil path instead. Beyond Phonak, RogerDirect is also included in the latest hearing aids from its Sonova sister brands Unitron and Hansaton - and, on the implant side, in the Advanced Bionics Naida CI M and Sky CI M sound processors.

Installation on this path is genuinely an at-home job for the Roger 3 generation: the unlimited receivers install from the microphone itself, held next to the device. One condition applies to stereo: per Sonova's launch material, stereo sound requires a binaural pair of hearing aids with RogerDirect - a single aid, or a non-RogerDirect device, receives the signal without the stereo image.

Cochlear implant users - a first-class audience, not a footnote

Advanced Bionics is a Sonova company, and its current processors sit inside the same wireless family as Phonak's aids. The Naida CI M (the current adult processor) and the Sky CI M (the paediatric equivalent) both take RogerDirect - the same install-from-the-microphone route as a Phonak aid - and both can alternatively use the NeckLoop path via T-coil. Phonak's user guide for this microphone names the case directly: with a pair of RogerDirect hearing aids, or with a Naida CI M, the wearer benefits from stereo listening.

Older Advanced Bionics processors do not have RogerDirect, but most are not shut out: Phonak's compatibility tables route the Naida CI Q series through a design-integrated Roger 17 receiver, the NeckLoop, or Roger X - with the condition Phonak's own table attaches, that Roger X on the Q series must be used with a ComPilot; Harmony and Auria through Roger X with the iConnect adapter or the NeckLoop; and Neptune through Roger X with Neptune Connect. Selected processors from Cochlear and MED-EL have their own design-integrated and Roger X routes in the same tables. For a bimodal fitting - a hearing aid on one ear and an implant on the other - the pieces are individually covered where each device is RogerDirect-capable, but the manufacturer literature we reviewed does not describe bimodal Roger use as a single configuration, so have your audiology team confirm how your specific combination takes the signal.

Paths 2 and 3: everyone else, including other brands

Roger is not a Phonak-only system on the receiving end. Phonak's stated compatibility floor is that Roger works with virtually every hearing aid and cochlear implant that has a direct audio input or a T-coil - and "virtually" is the honest word, because the exceptions are real and covered below. A T-coil device from any manufacturer can wear the Roger NeckLoop. A device with a Direct Audio Input port or audio shoe takes the plug-in Roger X, and so does a device whose own manufacturer offers a Europlug-compatible streaming accessory - Phonak's third-party tables name examples such as the Oticon Streamer Pro, the GN ReSound Multi Mic and the Starkey Remote Microphone +. One practical caution on this route: where a Roger X is to be used, receiver installation and licence handling on older setups involves the Roger Installer, a tool Phonak enumerates for hearing care professionals rather than consumers - so budget an audiologist visit rather than assuming a kitchen-table job.

For NHS wearers the path splits by generation. The NHS Phonak Nathos Nova line is built on the Marvel platform and takes the RogerDirect route - it has no Direct Audio Input port at all, so the older audio-shoe route does not apply to it. The earlier Nathos Auto line is the reverse: Roger X with an audio shoe, or the T-coil and NeckLoop route. Any NHS aid with a T-coil, from any maker, can use the NeckLoop.

Who it is not for

Some devices have no Roger path at all: no T-coil, no Direct Audio Input or compatible streaming accessory, and no RogerDirect platform. A device must miss all of these before Roger is ruled out, but the population is not empty - it concentrates among small in-canal customs and many low-cost over-the-counter aids, where the T-coil is omitted for size or cost and no accessory streamer exists. Bluetooth does not rescue these cases: an aid's consumer Bluetooth is not a Roger receiver. If your model might sit in this group, confirm with your audiologist before spending anything.

A different group can use Roger On but would be better served elsewhere in the range. If your difficult listening is overwhelmingly seated meetings, the Roger Select 3 is purpose-built for the middle of a table, switching automatically between up to six participants - our full guide to it covers when it beats the Roger On. If your only problem is the television, a dedicated TV streamer such as the Phonak TV Connector solves that one job without a microphone attached - our guide to it covers the hookup detail. And if what you find second-hand is an earlier Roger On or Roger On iN, remember that the unlimited built-in receivers and the newest modes belong to the third generation only - an earlier unit is not this product at a discount.

Questions worth asking before you buy

Do I need to buy a receiver as well as the microphone?

Not if your devices are on a RogerDirect platform - Phonak Infinio, Lumity, Paradise or Marvel, the latest Unitron and Hansaton aids, or an AB Naida CI M or Sky CI M processor. The Roger On 3 carries unlimited receivers and installs them itself, and Phonak publishes no limit on how many devices one microphone can serve. Everyone else needs one physical receiver per configuration: NeckLoop, Roger X, or a design-integrated receiver.

What about older Phonak models?

Phonak platforms that predate RogerDirect connect through the other paths - the NeckLoop where there is a T-coil, or Roger X where there is a Direct Audio Input port or audio shoe. Phonak also makes design-integrated receivers shaped for selected Phonak hearing aids, which your audiologist can match to the exact model. The right path is model-specific, so confirm before purchasing.

I have an Advanced Bionics cochlear implant. Does this work with my sound processor?

With the current processors, yes, directly: Naida CI M and Sky CI M both take RogerDirect from this microphone, with no external receiver. Older AB processors - Naida CI Q series, Harmony, Auria, Neptune - lack RogerDirect but have documented routes through design-integrated receivers or Roger X with the matching adapter (a ComPilot for the Q series, iConnect for Harmony and Auria), and T-coil models can use the NeckLoop. Neptune is the exception there: Phonak's table gives it no NeckLoop route at all - its documented path is Roger X with the Neptune Connect adapter only. For bimodal fittings, confirm the exact combination with your audiology team, as the published material treats each side separately.

Can it stream my TV?

Yes - seated in its docking station, which takes the television's optical or analog audio output (both cables come in the box), the Roger On streams the television to whatever receivers it serves, and a cable into a smartphone does the same for music. Less well known: connected to a computer by USB it works like a headset for online calls, streaming the far end to your ears and returning your own voice through the microphone to the computer.

Does it work with hearing aids from other manufacturers?

Yes, provided there is a path in: any manufacturer's aid with a T-coil can use the NeckLoop, and aids with Direct Audio Input or a Europlug-compatible streamer from their own maker - Phonak's tables name the Oticon Streamer Pro, GN ReSound Multi Mic and Starkey Remote Microphone + among others - can use Roger X. A second-hand caution on that receiver: Phonak's 2023-era receiver documentation records a serial-number cutoff for the Roger X (the current edition no longer restates it) - older units cannot take RogerDirect installation - so check a used unit's serial before buying; our receiver guide covers this in depth. The microphone does not care whose logo is on the receiving aid; the receiver path is the whole question.

Is the third generation really different from the earlier Roger On?

Yes, materially. Sonova's launch material states the unlimited built-in receivers are only available in the Roger 3 generation, alongside features new at that launch such as stereo wide pointing mode. The earlier iN variant carried built-in receivers to install but not an unlimited pool, and pre-iN units needed separately purchased receivers installed professionally. When comparing prices across listings, first establish which generation is in front of you.
