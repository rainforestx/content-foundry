Phonak Roger Select 3: the table microphone for seated groups, and what "up to six" really means

The Phonak Roger Select is a wireless microphone shaped like a small puck, made to sit in the middle of a table and carry the voices around it past noise, straight into hearing aids and cochlear implant processors, Advanced Bionics processors among them. The third generation ships with unlimited receivers inside the microphone, follows whichever of up to six seated talkers is speaking, and also takes phone calls over Bluetooth. This guide explains each claim precisely, including the two most often misread.

What this guide covers. The current, third-generation Roger Select - the unit Phonak sells today, marketed simply as "Roger Select", with the 3 marking the generation in Phonak's launch material. It is written for people who wear Phonak or Unitron hearing aids on a recent platform, for Advanced Bionics cochlear implant users, and for wearers of other manufacturers' devices working out whether Roger can reach them at all. Earlier Select versions appear only where the differences matter - mostly to second-hand buyers. This is an editorial guide, independent of the manufacturer; every product fact in it is sourced, and the sources are listed at the end.

In brief.

  • A table microphone for seated groups: placed in the middle, it follows whichever of up to six talkers around it is speaking and streams the voice into receivers in your hearing devices.
  • "Up to six" counts the talkers it switches between - it is not a limit on how many people can listen.
  • The third generation carries unlimited built-in receivers and installs them itself, the same mechanism as the Roger On 3.
  • Its Bluetooth serves the one use Phonak's professional reference gives it - phone calls; everything else the devices hear travels over the Roger link, which is not Bluetooth.
  • If your difficult listening moves around rather than staying seated, the Roger On 3 is usually the better fit.

What it is, and how it works

Start with the honest limitation this product exists to fix. Hearing aids do their best work close up; in a seated group - a meeting, a family dinner, a busy restaurant - the person you need to hear is often several seats away, and the aids' microphones take in the whole room. No hearing aid setting fully solves that, and Phonak's professional literature positions the Select accordingly: the ideal microphone for "stationary situations where background noise is present". If your difficult listening happens mostly while seated, this is the Roger microphone designed for you; if it happens on the move, it is not, and we say so plainly further down.

The Select works by getting a microphone into the middle of the group. Its round body carries what Phonak calls MultiBeam Technology: multiple microphones forming beams in six directions, covering speech from the full 360 degrees. It continuously compares the signal-to-noise ratio across the six directions and switches to the clearest one, so the pickup follows the conversation around the table without anyone touching anything. When two conversations run at once, you can override the automation: tap one of the six segments and the microphone locks onto that direction until released - useful when the one quiet voice you care about is losing to a louder corner.

It is not only a table device. A built-in accelerometer senses its orientation - lying flat, or hanging from a lanyard - and selects the microphone mode accordingly. Worn by a presenter it becomes a lapel-style microphone, holding the speaker's voice however far away they stand. And if dropped, it mutes on the way down, sparing the listener the bang of the landing.

Two radios in one device: the Roger link and the Bluetooth extra

This is the point most worth slowing down for, because the Select carries two radios doing different jobs. The link that carries sound into your hearing devices is Phonak's own Roger protocol - a 2.4 GHz radio system that broadcasts each packet of sound three times on different channels and hops frequencies to dodge interference. Your hearing aids or implant processors receive it through a Roger receiver, and only through a Roger receiver. Separately, the current Select also has Bluetooth, put to the one use Phonak's professional reference gives it: wireless phone calls. That Bluetooth leg runs between your phone and the microphone; the leg into your ears stays Roger. What it does not mean - the misreading to avoid - is that hearing aids with their own Bluetooth can receive this microphone's signal. An aid's consumer Bluetooth is not a Roger receiver.

The receiver side, briefly

A Roger microphone is a transmitter, and on its own it does nothing: the sound has to land in a receiver in or on your hearing devices. That receiver story is identical across the current Roger microphones; our receiver guide treats every path in depth, and the Roger On 3 guide summarizes all four. The short version: the Select 3 belongs to the Roger Unlimited generation Sonova launched in August 2024, which builds unlimited RogerDirect receivers into the microphone itself. If your aids are on a compatible platform, you hold them next to the microphone once, a receiver installs wirelessly into each aid, and there is nothing extra to buy or wear. The connection is remembered - day to day you just switch the microphone on. Other devices connect through a separate physical receiver, covered under compatibility below.

Living with it: the six-talker question, sharing, and the TV

Phonak's product page for the Select describes it serving "small group conversations with up to six participants", and that figure is the most misread number on the product. Six is not a limit on how many people can listen. It describes the pickup: six beam directions, so up to six seated talkers the automation can distinguish and switch between. On the listening side, Phonak publishes no fixed limit in its consumer guides on how many hearing aids or processors one Select can serve. Several listeners can share one microphone in the same meeting: each receiver goes through the one-time connect, and everyone hears the same feed. The question for a family table or a team is never "are we under six listeners" - it is which receiver path each listener's devices take.

Beyond the table there are two more everyday roles. Docked in the Roger docking station, the Select becomes a TV streamer, sending the television's sound to whatever receivers it serves; a cable into a smartphone does the same for music. For rooms bigger than one microphone can cover, the Select supports Phonak's MultiTalker Network, which lets several Roger microphones work together. How big such a network can grow is a figure we could not verify for the Select 3: the consumer surfaces searched for this guide (Rogerpedia, the Roger On 3 user guide and Phonak's product pages) state no cap, while Phonak's technical datasheet for the Roger On 3 puts that unit's network at up to ten connected microphones. Neither the silence nor the ten can be assumed to carry over to the Select, so plan a multi-microphone setup with your audiologist.

One honesty note a spec-minded reader deserves: Phonak publishes a technical datasheet for the Select 3, but we could not verify a copy against the original at the time of writing, and the secondhand copies we found disagree on details. So this guide quotes no battery-life, weight, dimension, charging or range figures for this unit - not because they do not exist, but because we will not pass on numbers we could not check. Nor do the earlier Select's figures transfer: quoting the old unit's range or battery on the new one is exactly the error this guide exists to avoid.

Compatibility, precisely

Compatibility is one question wearing many faces: by what receiver path can your devices take the signal? For the Select 3, three answers between them cover the field.

RogerDirect, the built-in route. Phonak hearing aids on the Infinio, Lumity and Paradise platforms - the current ranges - include RogerDirect, as does Marvel, an earlier platform still supported. The unlimited receivers in the Select 3 install straight into these aids, with nothing worn and nothing more to buy. One fine-print exception in Phonak's literature: several Virto custom in-ear models, the Titanium builds and certain variants supplied without wireless, are excluded from RogerDirect and connect by T-coil instead. The latest Unitron aids, from Phonak's Sonova sister brand, also carry RogerDirect - as, per the catalogue research behind this guide, do the equivalent Hansaton models. And per Phonak, stereo sound only works with hearing aids with RogerDirect.

Cochlear implant users - named, not footnoted. On the implant side, RogerDirect is included in the Advanced Bionics Naida CI M (the current adult sound processor) and Sky CI M (the paediatric one) - Advanced Bionics is a Sonova company, in the same wireless family. Both take the same install-from-the-microphone route as a Phonak aid, and both can alternatively use the NeckLoop via T-coil. Older AB processors lack RogerDirect but mostly keep a route in: Phonak's compatibility tables run the Naida CI Q series through a design-integrated Roger 17 receiver, Roger X (which the same table notes must be used with a ComPilot) or the NeckLoop; Harmony and Auria through Roger X with the iConnect interface or the NeckLoop; and Neptune through Roger X with Neptune Connect only - no NeckLoop route is listed for it. Selected Cochlear and MED-EL processors have their own documented routes in the same tables. For a bimodal fitting - aid on one ear, implant on the other - the published material treats the two sides separately, so have your audiology team confirm how your combination takes the signal.

Everything else. Phonak's stated floor is that Roger is compatible with virtually every hearing aid and cochlear implant that has a direct audio input, a T-coil or RogerDirect - and "virtually" is the manufacturer's own honest hedge, kept here deliberately. A T-coil device from any manufacturer wears the Roger NeckLoop; a device with a Direct Audio Input port or audio shoe, or a compatible streamer from its own maker, takes the plug-in Roger X. NHS wearers split by model: the current NHS Phonak Nathos Nova line is Marvel-platform and takes RogerDirect; the earlier Nathos Auto goes the Roger X or T-coil route. The receiver-path detail, including second-hand Roger X cautions, is covered in the Roger On 3 guide and applies to the Select unchanged.

The earlier Selects, because the second-hand market is full of them

Two earlier versions of this product exist and look similar in photographs. The original Roger Select had Bluetooth phone calls and a docking station but no receivers on board - receivers were bought and installed separately. The Roger Select iN, discontinued by Phonak in 2024 but still supported, carries exactly two RogerDirect receiver licences: enough for one pair of compatible aids, installed by holding each aid within 10 centimetres of the microphone, after which the pool is empty - a third aid means uninstalling from one of the first two. The iN also has no Bluetooth. Phonak's naming implies a version ladder, but its published material does not number the earlier units, so nor do we - what matters is which of the three units is in front of you, and its licence state if it is an iN.

Who it is not for

Some devices have no Roger path at all - no RogerDirect platform, no T-coil, no Direct Audio Input, no compatible streamer. Most of the devices in that position are tiny in-canal customs or budget over-the-counter aids, categories where the T-coil is the first casualty of size and cost; and for the reason drawn above, the aid's own Bluetooth cannot substitute. Anyone unsure which side of this line their model falls on should put the question to an audiologist before any money changes hands.

A second group can use the Select but would be better served elsewhere in the range - the honest test is where your difficult listening physically happens. If it moves - one-to-one conversations on the go, pointing at a talker across a bar, online calls from a computer - the Roger On 3 is the better fit: pointing mode 2.0, stereo wide pointing and headset mode are, per Phonak's current literature, Roger On capabilities the Select does not have. If your meetings are large, long-table affairs, the Roger Table Mic 3 is built for that scale. And if the television is your only problem, a dedicated TV streamer solves that one job without a microphone attached. The Select earns its place when the seated group is the recurring problem.

Questions worth asking before you buy

Is the Roger Select a hearing aid?

No - it is a wireless microphone, and it does nothing on its own. It transmits speech to Roger receivers in hearing aids or cochlear implant processors you already wear, which is why the receiver-path question matters more than any feature. If you do not yet wear hearing devices, it has nothing to connect to.

Can more than six people listen through one Select?

Yes. Six counts talkers, not listeners: it is the number of beam directions the microphone can switch between around a table. On the listening side Phonak states no fixed limit in its published consumer guides - each listener's receiver connects once, and everyone connected hears the same feed. What each extra listener does need is a receiver path of their own: RogerDirect aids install from the microphone's unlimited pool; other devices each need their own physical receiver.

Do I need to buy a receiver as well as the microphone?

Not if your devices are RogerDirect-capable: Phonak Infinio, Lumity, Paradise or Marvel aids, the latest Unitron models, or an AB Naida CI M or Sky CI M processor. The Select 3 carries unlimited receivers and installs them itself, once per device. Older Phonak platforms and other manufacturers' devices connect through a physical receiver instead - NeckLoop for any T-coil device, Roger X for direct-audio-input devices - and the right one is model-specific, so confirm your exact model before buying. Our Roger On 3 guide covers the four paths in detail.

My hearing aids already stream from my phone by Bluetooth. Why is that not enough?

Because they are different links doing different jobs. Your aids' Bluetooth streams from your phone; Roger is a separate radio protocol built to carry a distant talker's voice through noise, received only by a Roger receiver. The Select's own Bluetooth does not change this: it runs between your phone and the microphone, for calls, while your aids receive everything over the Roger link. If your aids lack a Roger path, no amount of Bluetooth connects them to this microphone.

I have an Advanced Bionics cochlear implant. Will the Select work with my processor?

Yes - and if yours is a current processor, no extra hardware is involved: the Naida CI M and Sky CI M both include RogerDirect and install from the microphone the same way a Phonak aid does, with the NeckLoop as a T-coil alternative. Older AB processors have documented routes with hardware: Naida CI Q series via a design-integrated Roger 17, Roger X (used with a ComPilot, per Phonak's table) or NeckLoop; Harmony and Auria via Roger X with iConnect or NeckLoop; Neptune via Roger X with Neptune Connect only. For some older processors Phonak's tables also document recommended CI profile settings for the receiver connection - detail your audiology team will recognise. Bimodal wearers should confirm their specific combination with that team, as Phonak's material covers each side separately.

Should I buy the Roger Select or the Roger On?

Buy for where your listening actually breaks down. Seated groups are the Select's home ground: six-segment steering, tap-to-lock on one voice, and Bluetooth phone calls. Varied and mobile situations favour the Roger On, whose pointing mode 2.0, stereo wide pointing and headset mode the Select does not have. Both stream TV and multimedia, both join MultiTalker networks, and both carry unlimited built-in receivers, so neither choice costs you the receiver advantage.

Is a second-hand Roger Select a sensible buy?

It can be, if you establish which unit you are looking at before money moves. A used Select 3 still carries its unlimited licence pool, so it can install into your compatible aids. A Roger Select iN carries exactly two licences - if the previous owner installed both, the pool is empty until a licence is uninstalled from their aids, so ask about licence state, not just condition. The original Select carries no installable licences and needs separately purchased receivers. The iN was discontinued in 2024 but remains supported - not obsolete, simply not this product, and it should not be priced as if it were.

Is it worth the money?

For the right person, yes - and the right person is specific: someone whose recurring listening failures are seated and group-shaped, who wears compatible devices, and for whom aids alone demonstrably run out of reach across a table. If your problem is occasional, mobile, or mostly the television, better-fitting answers exist above. Before any private purchase, check the funded routes: working-age UK adults in employment can ask about the government's Access to Work scheme, and parents of deaf children can ask their local authority about radio aid provision through education services - in each case, ask whether the scheme covers Roger equipment. No verified UK retail price was available at the time of writing, so this guide quotes none.
